When a leak is an emergency

A pinhole leak can run for months with little visible harm. A fast one can soak insulation, ceiling drywall and a finished basement within hours. Treat it as an emergency when water is visible and growing, when you hear running water with every tap closed or when a wall or ceiling is sagging.

First steps for an active leak

  • !Turn off the main water valve, usually where the line enters the house near the meter
  • !Open a low faucet to relieve pressure and drain the lines
  • !Move belongings and place a bucket or towels under the drip
  • !Stay clear of ceilings that look sagging or wet near light fixtures
  • !Photograph the damage, since your insurer will ask

No single tool finds everything. A good technician combines two or three, and confirms the result before anyone cuts into a wall or breaks concrete.

High water bill: the meter test

A bill that jumps with no change in habits is often the first sign of a leak. The simple test is to turn off every fixture, appliance and irrigation system, then look at the meter. If the leak indicator or the dial keeps moving, water is escaping somewhere between the meter and the fixtures.

Mahwah's water system is run through the township, and billing questions go to its Water/Sewer Billing and Collections office. Check whether your own account shows a recent jump, then call us for the meter test and a leak search before you assume the bill is wrong.

The usual culprits are a running toilet, a leaking outdoor spigot, an irrigation valve stuck open and a leaking service line between the street and the house. A toilet that fills on its own is the most common and the easiest to fix, which is why toilet repair is often the first stop.

Slab leaks and under-floor leaks

A slab leak is a leak in a supply line that runs under or inside a concrete floor. Most Mahwah houses have basements, so slab leaks are less common than in slab-on-grade regions, but they do occur under additions, garages and slab sections of split-levels. Signs include a warm spot on the floor, the sound of running water with fixtures off and unexplained moisture at a wall base.

Detection uses acoustic equipment and thermal imaging to find the line, then the repair is chosen: open and patch the pipe, reroute the line through the walls or, in extreme cases, repipe the affected branch.

Leaks between the street and the house

A leak on the service line, the pipe that brings water from the main to your house, may never show inside the home. It can saturate the soil, show as a wet patch in the yard or lower pressure at every tap. Locating it involves acoustic listening on the line and, when needed, excavating a small area. When the line itself has failed, the fix is covered under water service line repair and replacement.

Codes and standards that apply

Leak repairs fall under the same plumbing code as new work, since New Jersey adopts the National Standard Plumbing Code through N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15. A repair that opens a wall or slab may need an inspection afterward, and permit rules vary, so ask the local construction office. Keep the plumber's written report, which many insurers ask for.
