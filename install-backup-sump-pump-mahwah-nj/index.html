How to Install a Backup Sump Pump in Mahwah, NJ

A backup sump pump is a second pump that takes over if the main pump fails or the power goes out. In a river valley like Mahwah, where heavy rain raises the water table and storms often cut power, a backup is the difference between a dry basement and a flooded one.

If the pit is filling and your pump has stopped now, check the power and the float, and call. Install the backup after the emergency.

Why a backup matters

Sump pumps fail for two main reasons. The pump itself wears out, and the power goes off during the storm that fills the pit. A single pump protects against neither. A backup that runs from a battery or water pressure keeps working through both.

Many homes combine a good primary pump with a battery backup. In an area where outages come with storms, that pairing is common.

Sizing and setup

  • The backup should be sized to the same job as the main pump, or somewhat smaller if the main is oversized.
  • The pit must be large enough for two pumps and two floats, with the backup float set slightly higher than the main.
  • A separate discharge pipe or a connection with its own check valve keeps the two pumps from pushing water back into each other.
  • The battery sits in a vented, accessible place above the floor, and the charger plugs into a GFCI outlet.

How a backup installation goes

  1. Inspect the pit and the main pump

    The plumber checks the pit size, the existing pump, the check valve and the discharge.

  2. Set the backup pump

    It is placed beside or above the main pump, with its own float and check valve.

  3. Connect the discharge

    The backup discharge joins the main discharge above the main check valve, or runs separately.

  4. Install the battery and charger

    The battery is set in a box, and the charger and controller are wired.

  5. Test both pumps

    Water is poured into the pit to start each pump in turn, and the alarm is tested.

Our page on sump pump installation and repair describes the full service. For a basement that stays damp even with a pump, basement waterproofing is the next step.

Test it twice a year

Pour a bucket of water into the pit and watch each pump start, empty the water and stop. Unplug the main pump and test the backup separately. Replace the battery on the manufacturer's schedule, and check the discharge outlet outside after storms and in winter for ice.

Alarms and monitoring

A backup pump that nobody knows has failed is not a backup. A high water alarm in the pit sounds when the water rises above the normal level, and a battery charger with an indicator shows when the battery needs attention.

  • Add a high water alarm that sounds in the house, not only in the basement.
  • Consider a monitor that sends an alert to your phone, which helps if you travel.
  • Label the breaker for the pump so anyone in the house can find it.
  • Plug pumps into a GFCI outlet and not an extension cord, and do not share the outlet with the freezer or the washer.
  • Keep a spare float switch and a spare check valve on the shelf.

Test the whole system twice a year. Pour a bucket of water into the pit until the float lifts, confirm the main pump runs, then unplug it and confirm the backup starts. Do this before spring and again before fall, and write the date on the pump with a marker so the next check is easy to remember.

Codes and standards that apply

Check the pump's rating plate and the battery maker's guidelines for runtime. New Jersey adopts the National Standard Plumbing Code through N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15. Where the discharge may go is a local matter, so ask the local construction office.

Mahwah flood and discharge notes

The National Weather Service lists a flood stage of 8 feet at the Ramapo River gauge at Mahwah, and its historical list shows a highest crest of 15.77 feet in August 2011. The valley's glacial sand and gravel aquifer, which the township's wellhead protection ordinance describes, lets groundwater rise quickly after heavy rain.

Discharge rules differ by municipality, and some prohibit sump water in the sanitary sewer, so ask the township where your pump should discharge. Permit rules vary, so ask the local construction office about electrical work for a new outlet.
