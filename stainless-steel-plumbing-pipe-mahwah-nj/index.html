Stainless Steel Plumbing Pipe: Mahwah, NJ Homeowner Guide

Stainless steel plumbing pipe is strong, long lasting and resistant to corrosion, and it is also uncommon in houses. This guide explains where it is used, how it compares with copper and PEX and what to ask before choosing it for a repipe or a remodel.

There is also stainless steel gas tubing, which is a different product with its own rules. We cover that near the end.

What stainless steel pipe is

Stainless steel is steel alloyed with chromium and often nickel, which forms a thin protective layer that resists rust. Two grades appear in plumbing. Type 304 is the common general-purpose grade. Type 316 adds molybdenum for better resistance to chlorides, which matters near salt or in water with high chloride levels.

Stainless is used in food processing, hospitals, laboratories, pharmaceutical plants and commercial buildings with demanding water. In houses, it appears in special cases such as a very corrosive supply, a design that must last decades without service or an exposed installation where the look matters.

For most houses, copper or PEX do the job at a lower cost. Stainless makes sense only when the water or the application justifies the extra expense.

Joints and installation

Stainless is joined with press fittings that use a special tool and sealing rings, or with welded joints. Both need installers trained for the system. A poor joint is a leak, and the pipe itself is not the weak point. Ask whether the plumber has experience with the specific press system before agreeing.

Stainless steel gas tubing is a different thing

Corrugated stainless steel tubing, called CSST, is a flexible yellow or black-jacketed tubing for natural gas and propane. It is not used for water. The manufacturers publish installation and bonding requirements, and code officials enforce them. Gas work should be done by a qualified plumber and inspected where required.

Questions to ask before choosing stainless

  • Why stainless over copper or PEX for this house?
  • Which grade, and does the water chemistry support it?
  • Which press system and fittings will be used, and are they rated for potable water?
  • Who has done this system before, and what is the warranty on the joints?
  • Will the local code official accept the system, and who handles the permit?

If you are considering a repipe, the broader choice of materials is covered on our page on repiping, and installation planning on plumbing installation.

Mixing metals and fittings

Joining stainless steel to copper, brass or galvanized pipe can cause galvanic corrosion, where one metal wears faster because of the electrical difference between them. Plumbers use the proper transition fittings, such as dielectric unions or press adapters, to limit the effect.

For most houses the simpler path is to stay with one material per system. A repipe in copper or PEX is easy to repair later because any plumber has the parts. A stainless system calls for a plumber who stocks the press fittings, so ask who will service it ten years from now.

If your concern is corrosion, a water test and a look at the pipe you have now often answer the question better than a change of material.

Before any change of material, ask what failed in the existing system. Pinhole leaks in copper, scale in galvanized steel and joint failures in old fittings each point to a different cause, and a new pipe material does not cure a water chemistry or installation problem. Write down what you see, photograph the damaged sections and keep the old pipe for the plumber to examine.

Ask for the fitting system in writing. Press, push and welded joints each need different tools and training, and a plumber unfamiliar with the system can leave a weak joint behind. Ask who made the fittings, what the warranty covers and whether the manufacturer certifies the installer. Those answers matter more than the pipe grade printed on the label.

Mahwah water notes for pipe choices

Mahwah's 2026 water quality report lists pH between 6.8 and 8.2 and hardness from 91 to 203 ppm, and it notes that the township adds a phosphate compound that forms a protective coating inside pipes. That treatment is designed to protect existing copper and other metal plumbing.

The report does not give chloride numbers in the part we reviewed, so if you are weighing 304 against 316 stainless, ask the township or Veolia for chloride data. New Jersey's plumbing subcode adopts the National Standard Plumbing Code, so ask the local construction office what is allowed.
