When a commercial plumbing problem is an emergency

For a business, the emergency often starts with the clock. A sewer backup during dinner service, a water heater failure in a salon or a burst pipe above a stockroom each carries a cost beyond the repair. The first goal is to stop the damage and the second is to reopen safely.

Immediate steps for a business

  • !Close the building's main water shutoff and note where it is for next time
  • !Stop using restrooms and kitchen sinks if the sewer is backing up
  • !Move inventory, paper records and equipment off the floor
  • !Turn off power to wet areas only if you can do it without standing in water
  • !Photograph the damage for the insurer and the landlord

Grease, backflow and storm drains

Grease traps and interceptors capture fats before they reach the sewer. When a trap is full, grease passes into the line and builds up, and backups follow. Cleaning on a regular schedule is cheaper than an emergency, and a camera pass of the kitchen line shows whether grease has already built up past the trap.

Backflow preventers on commercial water services protect the public water supply from contamination. They are typically tested on a schedule, and the water purveyor sets the requirements. Ask your water utility what applies to your service. Storm drains in lots and loading areas clog with leaves and debris, and cleaning them before heavy rain avoids ponding.

What to do before the plumber arrives

Safety steps

  • !Shut off the water at the main valve if a pipe has failed, and tell staff to avoid the wet area.
  • !Keep customers away from standing water, since wet floors and nearby outlets are a hazard.
  • !Do not use a drain that is backing up, and keep food service equipment off until it is cleared.
  • !Photograph the damage and note the time for your records and your insurer.
  • !Call the building owner or manager if the line may be shared with other tenants.

Codes and standards that apply

Commercial work is held to the same plumbing standards as homes, and often to extra requirements for the building type. New Jersey adopts the National Standard Plumbing Code through N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15. Permit and inspection rules vary, so ask the local construction office, and ask your insurer whether a backflow or grease rule applies to your business.
