How to Repair a Dripping Bathroom Faucet: What Mahwah, NJ Homes Need to Know

A dripping bathroom faucet wastes water and wears out the sink finish, and in most cases the fix is a small, inexpensive part and not a new fixture. The repair depends on the faucet type, so the first step is working out which one you have.

If the faucet will not shut off at all, close the small valve under the sink and call. That is an emergency and no longer a drip.

Find out which faucet you have

Four designs cover most bathroom faucets. The repair is different for each, and ordering the wrong part is the commonest reason a repair fails.

Faucet type, how to spot it and what wears
TypeHow to spot itWhat usually wears
CompressionTwo separate handles that screw downRubber washers and the brass seat
CartridgeOne handle or two, smooth action with a stem insideO-rings and the cartridge
BallA single handle on a rounded capSeats, springs and the ball
Ceramic discA single handle with a wide cylindrical bodySeals and the disc cylinder

Shut off the water first

Look under the sink for two small valves, one for hot and one for cold. Turn them clockwise until they stop. Open the faucet to drain the lines and confirm that no water flows. Plug the drain so small screws cannot fall into it.

If a valve will not turn or leaks at the stem, do not force it. A seized valve can break, and a plumber can replace it with the faucet. See our page on faucet repair for how we handle those.

Repairing the drip, by type

  1. Remove the handle

    Pry off the cap or loosen a small set screw, then lift the handle off the stem.

  2. Take out the cartridge or stem

    A retaining nut or clip holds it. Note the orientation so the new one goes in the same way.

  3. Replace the worn part

    Washers, O-rings, seats or the whole cartridge. Take the old part to the store to match it.

  4. Clean the valve body

    Wipe out mineral deposits with a cloth and vinegar.

  5. Reassemble and test

    Turn the supply valves back on slowly and check for drips at the spout and around the base.

Hard water makes drips worse

Minerals in hard water settle on the cartridge, the O-rings and the aerator. They roughen sealing surfaces so a part that should last years starts leaking sooner. If your replacement cartridge begins dripping within a year or two, scale is a likely cause.

  • Unscrew the aerator and soak it in white vinegar to dissolve scale and restore flow.
  • Wipe the spout and base with a vinegar-dampened cloth to protect the finish.
  • Consider a whole-house filter or conditioner if scale is visible on every fixture.

Repair or replace the faucet

Repair

When the body is sound, the finish is good and parts are available.

Replace

When the body is pitted, the finish is peeling or parts are discontinued.

Replace the valves too

If the shutoff valves are old, change them while the faucet is off.

Call a plumber

When the faucet is wall-mounted or the leak is behind the wall.

A drip that appears after the faucet has been repaired may be coming from the valve seat. Resurfacing or replacing the seat is a plumber's job. If you see staining on the ceiling below a bathroom, the leak may be bigger than a faucet, and leak detection is the next step.

Common mistakes that cause a second leak

  • Overtightening the packing nut or the retaining nut, which cracks the part or the body.
  • Installing a cartridge that looks similar but is a different model, so it never seals.
  • Forgetting to close the drain, then losing a small screw or washer down the sink.
  • Turning the water back on at full pressure with the faucet closed, which can blow debris into the new seat. Open the faucet first.
  • Swapping hot and cold sides during reassembly, which reverses the handle.

Take a photo of each step as you take the faucet apart. Lay the parts on a towel in the order they came off. If anything looks cracked, pitted or crusted with scale, replace it while it is open.

If the faucet is older than the rest of the bathroom, ask whether it is worth another repair. Parts for discontinued models can be hard to find, and a new faucet with a common cartridge makes later repairs easier. A plumber can tell you which path costs less over the next few years.

Codes and standards that apply

Replacement parts should meet the standard for drinking water contact, such as NSF/ANSI 61 marking on faucet parts that touch drinking water. Check the packaging before you buy. New Jersey adopts the National Standard Plumbing Code through N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15.

Mahwah water and faucets

Mahwah's 2026 water quality report lists hardness from 91 to 203 ppm, and about two thirds of the township's water comes from seven wells along the Ramapo River valley with the rest supplied by Veolia. That mix means hardness can differ from one address to another, so the amount of scale you see on a faucet is a good local test.

The same report shows the township adds a phosphate compound to protect pipes. It does not soften the water, so scale on aerators and cartridges is still the main wear factor for Mahwah faucets.
