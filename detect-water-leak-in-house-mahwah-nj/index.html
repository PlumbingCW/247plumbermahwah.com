How to Detect a Water Leak in Your House in Mahwah, NJ

Most hidden leaks give hints long before they cause serious damage. A bill that creeps up, a patch of peeling paint or a sound in the walls are all clues. This guide walks through the checks a homeowner can do in an afternoon, and the signs that mean it is time for a plumber.

If water is actively flowing where it should not, close the main water valve and call. The checks below are for slow leaks, not floods.

Signs of a hidden leak

Look for these clues

  • ✓A water bill that rises with no change in how you live
  • ✓The sound of running or hissing water when everything is off
  • ✓Musty smells, mold or peeling paint on walls and ceilings
  • ✓Warm spots on a floor, or a damp area on carpet
  • ✓Low water pressure at every tap
  • ✓A wet patch or unusually green grass in the yard

The meter test

This is the single best check. Turn off all fixtures and appliances, including the ice maker and any sprinklers. Read the water meter, then wait fifteen to thirty minutes without using water, and read it again. A change means a leak between the meter and the fixtures. A moving leak indicator, the small triangle or wheel on the dial, shows the same thing.

What a plumber adds

If the meter moves and you cannot find the source, a plumber uses listening equipment, thermal cameras and pressure tests to find it without tearing out walls. Those tools find leaks behind finished surfaces and under slabs. See our page on leak detection for the methods.

If the search points to the line from the street, water service line repair is the next step. If a toilet is the culprit, toilet repair usually fixes it fast.

Groundwater is not a leak

Dampness in a basement is not always a plumbing leak. After heavy rain, groundwater pushes through the wall or the floor joint. A basement that is damp only after storms points to drainage, not pipes, and the fix is a sump pump and interior drain. See basement waterproofing.

What to do when you find a leak

  1. Stop the water

    Close the fixture valve, or the main valve if you cannot isolate it.

  2. Document it

    Photograph the damage and the leak, and note the meter reading.

  3. Dry the area

    Use towels, fans and a dehumidifier. Wet drywall and insulation may need to come out.

  4. Call your insurer

    Ask what they require before repairs begin.

  5. Call a plumber

    Describe what you found. A good description speeds the visit.

Seasonal leak checks worth doing

  • Spring: open outdoor spigots, look for drips at the wall and check hoses for cracks after winter.
  • Summer: check irrigation zones for stuck valves and wet patches, and look at the water heater base.
  • Fall: disconnect hoses, shut off and drain outdoor lines and check the sump pump before the rains.
  • Winter: look for frost on exterior wall pipes, and keep a faucet dripping during a hard freeze.
  • After any storm: check the basement floor, the sump pit and the ceiling stains.

Photograph the water meter once a month. A habit of recording the number makes a slow leak easy to spot, because you see the pattern long before the bill shows it.

If you find a leak, stop the water first and look second. Close the fixture shutoff or the main valve, move belongings away from the wet area and take photos with the date visible. Those photos help with an insurance claim and help the plumber understand how far the damage has spread.

Codes and standards that apply

The meter test described here is the simple standard check: with every fixture off, a meter that keeps moving points to a leak somewhere past the meter. New Jersey adopts the National Standard Plumbing Code through N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15, which covers the repair once the leak is found.

Mahwah water and meter notes

Mahwah's water system is run by the township, and its Department of Public Works Water and Sewer office handles the system. Billing questions go to the Water/Sewer Billing and Collections office. If your bill and your meter reading do not match, ask them.

About two thirds of Mahwah's water comes from wells along the Ramapo River valley. Because the valley's water table rises with heavy rain, damp basement walls after storms are often groundwater and not a plumbing leak. The meter test separates the two.
