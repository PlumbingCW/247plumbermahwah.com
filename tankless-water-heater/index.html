What makes tankless installation different

A tankless heater needs a lot of energy at once. A gas unit can demand several times the gas flow of a tank heater, so the line from the meter, the regulator and the pipe sizing all have to be checked. An electric unit needs heavy circuits and enough panel capacity, which many older panels lack.

What has to be right before a tankless install

  • ✓Gas pipe sized for the unit's full burner input and the other appliances on the line
  • ✓Venting that matches the model, with a condensate drain for high-efficiency units
  • ✓Isolation valves so the heat exchanger can be flushed later
  • ✓A pressure and temperature setting that suits the household
  • ✓Enough incoming flow to meet the unit's minimum activation rate

Hard water and descaling

Hard water scale is the main enemy of tankless heaters. Mahwah's 2026 water quality report lists hardness between 91 and 203 ppm as calcium carbonate, which runs from moderately hard to very hard. Scale coats the heat exchanger, restricts flow and triggers error codes long before the unit actually fails.

Descaling means circulating a mild acid solution through the heat exchanger with a small pump, then flushing it. Manufacturers recommend doing this on a schedule, and many warranties assume it is done. Valves installed with the heater make the job a short one, and a unit without them needs fittings added first.

Is tankless right for your home?

A good fit

Homes with limited space, steady daily use and gas or electric service that can carry the load.

A poor fit

Homes where gas or electrical upgrades would be costly, or where a very large simultaneous demand is common.

Think about

The cost of gas line work, venting and condensate drainage, not just the price of the unit.

Ask for

A written estimate that lists the gas, venting and drain work separately.

Codes and standards that apply

Tankless heaters have strict venting, gas supply and clearance standards set by the manufacturer, and the installer must follow the installation manual for the model. New Jersey adopts the National Standard Plumbing Code through N.J.A.C. 5:23-3.15. Permit rules vary, so ask the local construction office about gas and venting work.

What to do before the plumber arrives

Safety steps

  • !If you smell gas, leave the house and call the gas utility from outside.
  • !If the unit shows an error code, write it down and do not keep resetting it.
  • !If a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, get everyone outside and call 911.
  • !Shut off the cold water to the unit if it is leaking, and turn off the gas at its valve.
  • !Do not touch the vent pipe, which can be very hot.
